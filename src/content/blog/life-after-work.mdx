## My last day of employment
After 37 years, on 25th May 2025, I stopped being paid by others to work.  I hope to have done my last day's work as an employee. 

Twelve months later, I find myself struggling on the morning jog if I've had a couple of beers the night before, wondering how I could sit at the dinner table but feel like I wasn't really there, getting distracted by a relatively continuous ringing in my ears and reflecting that I probably felt worse than I did when I had been working.  This was not what I had expected.   

I stopped working because I needed to.  In the months before, I had been diagnosed with stress related hypertension and didn't appreciate needing to be on medication simply to go to work. 

Very fortunately, I stopped work because I'd calculated that I could "afford" to.  Notwithstanding the possibility of WWIII, the maths showed that my family could thrive in any good scenario, drive through relatively easily in a median and survive in the worst case.  I recognise that others may not be so lucky.

And I stopped work because I wanted to.  I thought I would semi-retire at 56 and all would be good; I'd have time to play golf, learn to code, write, garden and spend more time with the family.  Most of all I looked forward to relaxing.

But experience is often the thing you get right after you need it.  I wish I had known the things I know now before I stopped working.  

If you're considering stopping work and you've been working hard for a long time then perhaps reading on will help you avoid some of the pitfalls I have encountered. 

## Careers, Jobs, and Gigging

My first paid employment was with Jaguar Motor Cars in 1988, the year I left school.  Since then, apart from a period waiting on tables and cleaning dishes in a golf club, I have worked in two different industry sectors - Automotive manufacturing and IT, specifically SAP implementation (I write elsewhere on this website about SAP).

I have heard it said that my parent's generation (the Boomers) would have one career, my generation (Gen X) would have five jobs in our lifetimes and our children's generation would end up having to do five jobs at the same time...and I concur with that prediction.

My Dad was a Doctor.  Full stop, end of his working story.  I've been employed by seven (admittedly not five, but close enough) different companies and my children are already running multiple gigs and they haven't even left school yet. My Mum was, perhaps, an exception in that she put herself through Open University, then Law School, to go from being a Nurse to being a Solicitor while never stopping being a full-time Mum.  

The general trend about the shape of work appears clear to me, but I wonder whether the impact on our minds and bodies as a result of this work-shifting will be the same?

## Fond Memories?

Along the way, I collected any number of memories - I list them here not to show off but because, at the time, they all felt like experiences that would mature into benign and fond memories -  
- In 1988 as part of Jaguar's training program, I was taught it's impossible to file a piece of metal perfectly flat

- At the end of 1992, I walked around a Ford Motor Company car park in Belgium counting how many Mondeos were missing their driver or passenger seats. I worked for the company that supplied the seat runners and, for many reasons, struggled to supply what Ford needed us to.  After a few weeks, seatless cars started appearing in Ford's car park and this led to their Site Director visiting our factory. He couldn't quite believe that I, a 23-year-old, was in charge of the (admittedly small) production facility and he left in a huff having told us to "pull our finger out".  To clear the backlog, we had to recruit, train, and run a temporary extra shift, co-ordinate emergency shipments of parts by land and air and generally run in crisis mode for a month or two. 
- As a Manufacturing Manager, I tried to reason with Shop Stewards twice my age only to find that they disliked talking to me just as much as the Ford site Director seemed to.
- I attended an interview for a job I knew nothing about (the newspaper advert literally said "Don't worry if you don't know what SAP is") and found that my two interviewers were late because they had been playing squash at lunchtime.  I got the job and was working in the States 18 months later. 
- I travelled business class (them sure were the good ole days!) to Philadelphia at the start of that stint in the States.  It was a day-flight, I travelled in a suit (I was a consultant after all) and got laughed out of SAP's Newtown Square offices as soon as I arrived.  I wore polo-shirts and chinos from that point forward.
- My wife and I spent 2-3 years each living in Boston (skiing at weekends in Vermont), San Diego, and Singapore - all part of a travel-the-world-while-implementing-SAP lifestyle.  We took advantage of each base location to visit even more wonderful places such as Hawaii, Chicago, Seattle, New Orleans, Montreal, Cambodia, Vietnam, Australia, Malaysia, and Indonesia.  
- For six months, I swam most mornings in the 1938 Empire Games pool right under Sydney's Harbour Bridge before going to work on a video-over-the-internet startup.  I think Netflix were still sending DVDs through the post at that time and their strategy definitely turned out to be better than ours.
- I lived and worked in the middle of London - ran round Battersea Park in the early morning, walked to and from work along the Thames Embankment, drank craft beers and ate curries in the evening. 
- Since 2010, I have helped bring up two wonderful children before we made the decision to leave London for many reasons but most of all because of Brexit, schooling, and space.    

Honestly, I loved most of it.  Most of the time.  
 
I consider myself very fortunate to have had the opportunities I have had and been remunerated well for my efforts.  Now, however, I wonder whether there were hidden costs and exactly what impact paying back the mind/body "debt" I seem to have accumulated will have on me and my family.

Looking back now, 12 months after my last pay-cheque, I wish I'd known more about how the body and mind do work together.  At the back of my mind, I probably knew that the chronic stress I was placing myself under wasn't entirely good for me but what has been genuinely surprising is the effect that taking that stress off has had after I stopped working.  

I expected to feel materially better but there have been many days in the 12 months since I stopped where I've gone "WTF have I done?".  

## What I wish I'd known before I stopped working

In all of my working life, the best piece of professional feedback I ever received was "Care less".  Two words, from my boss, about 10 years ago.

Why did he want me to "Care less"?  I'm not entirely sure because both of us chose to avoid talking through the feedback, but I think he was saying "watch out, you're always 'all in' and that may not end well".

Throughout my life, I followed my parents' example.  They both worked inordinately hard.  Sure, my sister and I lived a life off the back of the "fruits of their labour"; neither of us wanted for anything and that was largely down to Mum and Dad's prodigious work ethic.

Both my Mum (76) and Dad (86) have passed away in the time since COVID.  Luckily both led very healthy lives, right up to the point they didn't.  They both passed away as a result of heart attacks.  Up till leaving work last year, I didn't really think too much about the nature of their passing but have had time to reflect on it since leaving work.

I was wrong to think stopping work would be simple.  I thought "stop and I will feel better".  I stopped but felt worse and I wanted to find out why. 

## A bit more context first

Before going further - I should acknowledge that I have had (what a Surgeon General would call) an increasingly unhealthy relationship with alcohol since my teenage years.  Not increasingly unhealthy because my consumption increased (if anything it's decreased over the last ten years) but because the recommended "safe" amounts (or at least my perception of them) seems to have been reducing over time.  And, yes, I would often have a drink at the end of a stressful day at work.

I should also confess that I've never had a spa treatment or massage and never practised yoga, meditation or mindfulness.  Perhaps I'll end up wishing I had but, till now, I've thought I would manage just fine without any of that.  

Actually that's not entirely true.  I did one yoga session, which was led by my wife who is a qualified Iyengar yoga instructor.  At the end, she asked me how I found it.  I said "good but I don't think I have time for it".  She said "yes, that's the point".  

I didn't really understand what she meant but think I might now.

## The Let Down Effect

I had worked hard before a holiday, only to find that as soon as I relaxed I fell ill.  Apparently this is known as "The Let Down Effect".  

I had no inkling that in stopping work for good, I was setting myself up for a much more significant period of "Let Down".  

It's not as though I felt unwell every day for 12 months; rather, if I looked back over the whole year, I felt worse than I expected for much of it. 

For the first seven months, I continued on the course of (very low strength) betablockers that I had been prescribed for the stress-related hypertension and felt OKish.  
 
My Dad, a Doctor, had always said "avoid surgery if at all possible" and, by my observation, neither he nor my Mum took any kind of medication their whole lives.  I have been conditioned to see medication as the last resort.  

So, at the end of 2025, I decided that I didn't like the idea of being on medication and stopped taking them. 

About two to three weeks after stopping the betablockers, I started: 
- Noticing a relatively persistent low-level ringing in my ears that has become a consistent (unwelcome) companion.  It is not present all the time but definitely there in the quiet moments including when trying to relax in front of the TV.
- Feeling relatively minor irritating twinges and pains in my chest
- Noticing that I would sit at the dinner table with the family but not feel as though I was actually there
- Noticing that I could not run at my usual pace the day after I had drunk a couple of beers.  I'm not a fast "jogger" to begin with but, honestly, two beers the night before and I would have to stop after about 10 mins and simply had to walk.

I got checked out medically - nothing was obviously wrong.  The cardiologist specifically mentioned that I had a heart better than average and, based on how I presented, a 3% (very low) chance of a heart problem in the next ten years.  I had been to a sleep clinic in 2025 where I slept overnight in a hospital bed plugged into a machine that monitored my heart, breathing and god knows what else.  I was told I had mild sleep apnoea but it was mild enough not to need treatment.  

So, what was going on?  

## The Sympathetic and Parasympathetic Nervous Systems

I knew nothing of either the Sympathetic or Parasympathetic Nervous System.  My wife had read "The Body Keeps the Score" by Bessel van der Kolk but I had not.  

I had a sense of our evolutionary "fight or flight" response but had never heard of "rest and digest".  

I knew adrenaline was good at getting "us going" and cortisone was used to treat athletic injuries but not because cortisol is a natural anti-inflammatory.  I didn't know that chronic stress can lead to persistent overproduction of cortisol in a way that lessens the body's immune system's ability to counter inflammation.  And I certainly didn't connect that with the idea that inflammed blood vessels can increase your blood pressure and likely increase the chances of a stroke and/or heart attack.

I understood that alcohol was not good for me but never 
- internalised the depressant effect it could have been having over the years.  I've learned a new word in the last 12 months that I wish I could unlearn - [anhedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anhedonia).
- realised that, through regular drinking, the liver can be "trained" to increase the production of the enzymes used to break down alcohol.  And conversely, that this enzyme production is reduced when you stop or significantly reduce your drinking, which means that the liver can be caught unawares and unprepared should you happen on 2-3 days of drinking after a period off the booze.
- realised, the next-day dehydration that comes from drinking can thicken the blood in a way that leaves the body no choice but to increase blood pressure under exercise load and this can manifest itself by stopping you from running/jogging as you normal do. 

I didn't understand that betablockers have the effect of inhibiting the "fight or flight" response in a way that forces the body to increase the number of stress receptors in the body.  So that when you come off the medication the body can be left even more sensitive to stress than it was in the first place!

## So what's the point?

My main point is that if you have worked and played hard for a number of years and are considering stopping then please make sure you watch out for the "Let Down Effect" and be prepared for it.  

I know much more now than I did a year ago about how my body and mind work together.  As a result, I have to set myself the realistic expectation that it will take a while to get back to "normal" and that:
- I need to focus on the parasympathetic nervous system to help keep my sympathetic in check.  The ringing in the ears is simply normal levels of incoming stimuli being labelled as abnormal because the mind is on heightened threat alert and, in the absence of real threats, is hallucinating imaginary ones.  I don't know how I will do this yet but writing this post and building this website is part of that journey.  Who knows, maybe yoga and meditation will figure too but, not now, as I strongly believe either would only cause my brain to focus on the normal levels of incoming stimuli all the more.  
- I have to moderate (possibly with prolonged periods of abstinence) my use of both alcohol and caffeine
- I should keep sharing in the hope that I can find others who have experienced similar feelings.  I think most of this post could be labelled "looking after your mental health" and that we could all do better if we talked more openly about these feelings than perhaps we have in the past.

So, I record those learnings here in the hope that it may help someone else in a similar position feel better than I have done.  

I would have very much liked to have read about the lived experience of others who have experienced what I've experienced.  It would have helped me feel less worried and more "normal".

I hope learning to code some more, writing more content such as this post and improving my cryptic crossword solving ability (I can't stress enough how impressed I am by this site [Fifteensquared](https://www.fifteensquared.net/) if you're interested in learning how to decode clues; for puzzles published in the UK at least) will keep my cognitive functions fully engaged.  I have to find a way to ensure that my brain has no interest in monitoring normal noise levels and labelling them as abnormal.

I will update this post regularly as my own story unfolds.  Hopefully this will not be the last version of this particular story!
