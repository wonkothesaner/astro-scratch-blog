## Ending my life as an employee
After 38 years, on 25th May 2025, I stopped being paid by others to work.  I hope to have done my last day's work as an employee. 

I stopped because I needed to.  In the months before, I had been diagnosed with stress related hypertension and didn't appreciate needing to be on medication simply to go to work. Very fortunately, I had calculated that I could stop. 

Notwithstanding the possibility of WWIII, the maths showed that we could thrive in a good scenario, drive through easily enough in the median and survive in the worst case.  My family and I could still live happily without my monthly salary.  

I recognise that others may not be so lucky but I thought I would semi-retire at 56 and all would be good; I'd have time to play golf, learn to code, write, garden and spend more time with the family.  Most of all I looked forward to relaxing.

But experience is the thing you get right after you need it.  I wish I had known the things I know now before I stopped working.  If you're considering stopping work and you've been working hard for a long time then perhaps reading on will help you avoid some of the pitfalls I seem to have encountered since May 2025. 

## Careers, Jobs, and Gigging

My first paid employment was with Jaguar Motor Cars in 1988, the year I left school.  Since then, apart from a period waiting on tables and cleaning dishes in a golf club, I have worked in two different industry sectors - Automotive manufacturing and IT, specifically SAP implementation (I elsewhere on this blog about things SAP).

I have heard it said that my parent's generation (the Boomers) would have one career, my generation (Gen X) would have five jobs in our lifetimes and our children's generation would end up having to do five jobs at the same time...and I concur with that prediction.

My Dad was a Doctor.  Full stop, end of his working story.  

Over the years, I have had 7 employers, run my own business for a few years, and been part of one (unsuccessful) "start up".  

My two children are already simultaneously working multiple angles and they have not left school yet!  

Of course there are exceptions, my Mum put herself through Open University and then Law School to go from Nurse to Solicitor while never stopping being a full-time Mum.  

But the general trend appears clear to me, we all need to keep learning and keep moving.

However, looking back now, 12 months after my last pay-cheque, I wish I'd known more about how the body and mind work together and the impact de-stressing would have.  In fact I wish I had known that de-stressing would be such a poor description of what would happen.  There have been many days in the 12 months since stopping where I've gone "WTF have I done?".  

## What I wish I'd known before I stopped working

In all of my working life, the best piece of professional feedback I ever received was "Care less".  Two words, from my boss, about 10 years ago.

Why did he want me to "Care less"?  I'm not entirely sure because both of us chose to avoid talking through the feedback, but I think he was saying "watch out, you're always 'all in' and that may not end well".

Throughout my life, I followed my parents' example.  They both work inordinately hard.  Sure, my sister and I lived a life off the back of the "fruits of their labour"; neither of us wanted for anything and that was largely down to Mum and Dad's prodigious work ethic.

Both my Mum (76) and Dad (86) have passed away in the time since COVID.  Luckily both led very healthy lives, right up to the point they didn't.  They both passed away as a result of heart attacks.  Up till leaving work last year, I didn't really think too much about the nature of their passing but have had time to reflect on it since leaving work.

I was wrong to think it would be simple.  Stop and I would feel better.  I had stopped but felt worse.  I wanted to know why. 

## A bit more context first

Before going further - I've never had a spa treatment or massage and never practised yoga, meditation or mindfulness.  Perhaps I'll end up wishing I had but, till now, I've thought I would manage just fine without any of that.  

Actually that's not entirely true.  I did one yoga session, which was led by my wife who is a qualified Iyengar yoga instructor.  At the end, she asked me how I found it.  I said "good but I don't think I have time for it".  She said "yes, that's the point".  

I didn't really understand what she meant but think I might now.

Oh, and I should also acknowledge that I have had an increasingly unhealthy relationship with alcohol since being a teenager.  Not increasingly unhealthy because my consumption increased but because since the end of the 1980s, the recommended "safe" amounts (or at least my perception of them) has reduced over time whereas my consumption has remained relatively constant.  And, yes, I would often have a drink at the end of a stressful day at work.

_*Disclaimer*_ - I hope it's obvious already but none of what follows should be read as medical fact nor as advice.  My intention here is only to share my experiences in case they help others who may find themselves experiencing similar feelings.

## The Let Down Effect

I had worked hard before ahead of a holiday, only to find that as soon as I relaxed I fell ill.  Apparently this is known as "The Let Down Effect".  

I had no inkling that in stopping work for good, I was setting myself up for a much more significant period of "Let Down".  

It's not as though I felt unwell every day for 12 months more that, if I looked back over the whole year, I felt worse than I expected for much of it. 

For the first seven months, I continued on the course of (very low strength) betablockers that I had been prescribed for the stress-related hypertension and felt OKish.  Then at the end of the year, I decided that I didn't like the idea of being on medication and stopped taking them.  My Dad had always said "avoid surgery if at all possible" and, by my observation, neither he nor my Mum took any kind of medication their whole lives.  So I am conditioned to see medication as the last resort.  

About two to three weeks after stopping the betablockers, I started: 
- Noticing a relatively persistent low-level ringing in my ears that has become a consistent (unwelcome) companion.  It is not present all the time but definitely there in the quiet moments including when trying to relax in front of the TV.
- Feeling relatively minor irritating twinges and pains in my chest
- Noticing that I would sit at the dinner table with the family but not feel as though I was actually there
- Noticing that I could not run at my usual pace the day after I had drunk a couple of beers.  I'm not a fast "jogger" to begin with but, honestly, two beers the night before and I would have to stop after about 10 mins and simply had to walk.

I got checked out medically - nothing obviously wrong.  The cardiologist specifically mentioned that I had a heart better than average and, based on how I presented, a 3% (very low) chance of a heart problem in the next ten year.

So, what was going on?  

## The Sympathetic and Parasympathetic Nervous Systems

I knew nothing of either the Sympathetic or Parasympathetic Nervous System.  My wife had read "The Body Keeps the Score" by Bessel van der Kolk but I had not.  

I had a sense of our evolutionary "fight or flight" response but had never heard of "rest and digest".  

I knew adrenaline was good at getting "us going" and cortisone was used to treat athletic injuries.  

I understood that alcohol was not good for me but never 
- internalised the depressant effect it could have been having over the years
- understood that, through regular drinking, the liver can be "trained" to increase the produciton of the enzymes used to break down alcohol.  And conversely, that this enzyme production is reduced when you stop or significantly reduce your drinking, which means that the liver can be caught unawares and unprepared should you happen on 2-3 days of drinking after a period off the booze.
- realised, the next-day dehydration that comes from drinking can thicken the blood in a way that leaves the body no choice but to increase blood pressure under exercise load. 

I didn't understand that betablockers would "inhibit" the "fight or flight" response in a way that actually force the body to increase the number of stress receptors in the body.  So that when you come off the medication the body can actually even more sensitive to stress than it was before.

## So what's the point?

My main point is that if you have worked and played hard for a number of years and are considering stopping then please make sure you watch out for the "Let Down Effect" and be prepared for it.  

I know much more now than I did a year ago about how my body and mind work together.  As a result, I have to set myself the realistic expectation that it will take a while to get back to "normal" and that:
- I need to focus on the parasympathetic nervous system to help keep my sympathetic in check.  The ringing in the ears is simply normal levels of incoming stimuli being labelled as abnormal because the mind is on heightened threat alert and, in the absence of real threat, is hallucinating.  I don't know how I will do this yet but suspect it will not start with yoga or meditation as I suspect both will only cause the brain to focus on the normal levels of incoming stimuli all the more.  I need to find something that will keep my cognitive functions fully engaged so that the mind has no interest in monitoring the normal noise levels.
- I have to moderate (probably stop) my use of both alcohol and caffeine
- I should keep sharing in the hope that I can find others who have experienced similar feelings.  I think most of this post could be labelled "looking after your mental health" and that we could all do better if we talked more openly about these feelings than perhaps we have in the past.

So, I record those learnings here in the hope that it may help someone else in a similar position feel better than I have done.  

I would have very much liked to have read about the lived experience of others who have experienced what I've experienced.  It would have helped me feel less worried and more "normal".

I will update this post regularly as my own story unfolds.  Hopefully this will not be the last version of this particular story!
