This is not my first attempt at writing a post nor at building a website. &#x20;

Towards the end of the noughties, out of pure curiosity, I had purchased some [Head First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_First_\(book_series\)) books that let me dip a toe in the world of HTML and CSS.  Both of which felt very exotic to the dyed-in-the-wool SAP professional I was pretending to be at the time. &#x20;

In 2015 I decided to stop my SAP bus, get off and take a look around the "digital" world in more detail.  At the time, the world was a very different place compared to now:

* Barack Obama was still President in the US,
* the Tories were in power in the UK (but held back a little in coalition by the Liberal Democrats),
* Brexit was a dream for some rather than a reality for all,
* [Volodymyr Zelenskyy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volodymyr_Zelenskyy) was providing the voice for Paddington in movies rather than leading The Ukraine's resistance to Russian aggression.
* And everyone, except my SAP colleagues, where I worked (let's call them "**The Rest**") was talking about "digital transformation" and throwing arounds words like Jenkins, hybris, Continuous Integration, microservices - none of which I understood. &#x20;

I wanted to understand what **The Rest** were talking about.  Why?  Just in case they were describing a world and work that was more fun than that which I had become accustomed to.

I took a year out, took my children to and from nursery and school and filled the remaining spare time by trying to learn how Javascript works.  Why Javascript or (more properly) PascalCase JavaScript?  Because JavaScript seemed to be the thing that powered the majority of excitement around "digital transformation" at the time.  Especially if, in a lazy definition, one took "digital transformation" to mean delivering "better" websites.&#x20;

What I learnt back in 2015 was that there appeared to be no single and agreed right way to do pretty much anything at all.  The answer depended not just on what you wanted to do but also, very often, who you were prepared to listen to and then what you chose to believe. Many different people seemed to propose many different approaches all trying to achieve the same sort of results.  Angular and React were rising but one was a "library" and the other a "frameworks" - this seemed to matter to some but I honestly couldn't fathom why.&#x20;

In a world in which you could do something "this" way or "that" way, there were some who said "this" was better than "than" and vice versa.  In short, **opinions** seemed matter as much, if not more, than **facts**. &#x20;

I struggled with this enormously as I tend towards a worldview that we are collectively more likely to make "progress" more rapidly if we share a set of well understood and agreed facts rather than argue from perspectives based on opinions.  Of course, I recognise that agreeing on what is meant by "progress" is an important first step before doing anything and that the 2016 Brexit vote went on to challenge the idea that anyone could agree what "progress" was desired, in the UK if nowhere else.  Unfortunately I'd argue the trend has continued and that the actions of the 45th/47th President of the United States are based on opinions more than facts.  Perhaps Donald would disagree with the following?

> "Everyone is entitled to his own opinion, but not to his own facts."\
> <cite>Daniel Patrick Moynihan, United States Senator, 1927-2003</cite>

I digress.  Part of the JavaScript "opinions" beat "facts" challenge back in 2015 seemed to me to come from Douglas Crockford's book "[JavaScript: The Good Parts](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/javascript-the-good/9780596517748/)" in which the case is made that the language itself has some "quirks".

Shout out to Wes, Scott and CJ for the excellent work they do on Syntaxfm. I am sure there are other podcasts and blogs out there that look to do the same sort of things as Wes, Scott and CJ but I've yet to find one that does it as well as they do.
